\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) support a wide range of language tasks, but hallucinations remain a major obstacle to their reliable use. Their outputs may contradict established facts or introduce information unsupported by the available evidence. This survey presents an overview of hallucination in text-based LLMs, bringing together its definitions, detection methods, evaluation practices, and mitigation strategies. We begin by distinguishing world factuality from source faithfulness and illustrating common error types. We then examine detection approaches based on external evidence, sampling consistency, and internal model signals, alongside representative benchmarks and evaluation methods. We further discuss potential causes of hallucination and review interventions involving training, retrieval, decoding, and response revision. Through these comparisons, we examine the strengths and limitations of existing approaches and the trade-offs among factual accuracy, informative coverage, and computational cost. Finally, we discuss open challenges in evidence reliability, independent verification, and generalization, outlining directions for more reliable language generation. A companion repository of relevant literature and supporting materials is available at \url{https://github.com/lixincheng-xcl/LLM-Hallucination-Survey}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}
\textbf{RQ1} requires separating world factuality from source faithfulness and declaring the claim unit and unknown-label policy. For \textbf{RQ2}, external evidence enables support checks, whereas sampling and internal signals estimate reliability under distinct access and supervision constraints. For \textbf{RQ3}, useful mitigation requires independently verified reliability gains, assessed jointly with informative coverage and computational cost. Future work should test detector--intervention combinations under evidence conflicts and distribution shifts, linking mechanism choice to residual errors.
\section*{Limitations}
This narrative survey emphasizes English text tasks and ACL-family venues. Venue and keyword filters may omit relevant work; full-text checking was claim-specific rather than uniform. Heterogeneous generators, evidence, judges, and budgets prevent pooled effect estimates. The taxonomy and reporting protocol require prospective validation.
