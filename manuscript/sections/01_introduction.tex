\section{Introduction}
A fluent answer is not necessarily justified. In question answering (QA), a model may repeat a misconception; in summarization, it may add a plausible detail absent from the source. Both are called hallucinations, although their correctness criteria differ: source-unsupported content can be factually correct \citep{lin-etal-2022-truthfulqa,cao-etal-2022-hallucinated}. Collapsing these settings obscures what a system improves.

The cafe claim is unsupported, not necessarily false in the world. Visual organization adapted from \citet{liu-etal-2024-lvlm-design}; examples are original.}{fig:examples}

Figure~\ref{fig:examples} makes this distinction operational. The invented library record supports 12,000 books and a second-floor archive. The cafe assertion lacks support; the changed count and location contradict the record. The delivery summary similarly alters quantities and event order while inventing a donor. Verification must therefore specify both the claim and its reference evidence.

The field now spans claim verification, uncertainty estimation, and interventions throughout generation \citep{wang-etal-2024-factuality}. Their requirements differ: retrieval, repeated answers, or access to model internals. Lower error rates can also reflect shorter answers or more refusals. We organize this diversity around three questions: \textbf{RQ1}, what reference frame defines an error; \textbf{RQ2}, what evidence and access enable detection; and \textbf{RQ3}, when does mitigation improve useful generation?

\paragraph{Scope and organization.}
This targeted 2022--2026 survey emphasizes ACL-family work on text-based QA, long-form generation, summarization, dialogue, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG); it excludes multimodal perception and general reasoning.
